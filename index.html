Which Pages Should You Fix First? A Content-Prioritization Study

Maryum Akram · github.com/MaryumAkram16/flyrank-ml-internship

00 Abstract

Which pages should a content team review first, given limited weekly review capacity? This project builds and validates a content-prioritization pipeline on FlyRank's anonymized search-performance dataset, comparing a hand-tuned baseline rule against two machine-learning models under a client-holdout validation design. The central, counter-intuitive result: the simple baseline rule (Precision@50 = 0.90) outperformed a Random Forest (0.76) and matched Logistic Regression (0.92) — traced, via error analysis, to the model over-weighting high-traffic pages rather than the specific, independently confirmed CTR-vs-position signal the baseline targets directly. The output is a three-tier, human-reviewed action playbook, offered as decision-support rather than an automated system.

01 Introduction

A content team with real editorial capacity can typically review 20–50 pages a week. Out of an inventory of thousands, which pages deserve that attention first? This is a prioritization problem, not a pure prediction problem: the value of the output is measured by whether the top of a ranked list is trustworthy, not by overall accuracy across every page.

This study treats it that way from the start — a scoring/ranking task, evaluated with Precision@K rather than accuracy, built around one unit of analysis: one page, for one client, summarized over a trailing 90-day window.

02 Data

The primary dataset is the anonymized starter release (content_refresh_anonymized.csv) — 30,000 pages, 44 columns, one row per pseudonymized content item. After applying a 500-impression volume floor (excluding pages too small to trust a CTR measurement), 16,726 pages remained across 28 clients.

A separate exploration used the full warehouse release on Hugging Face (FlyRank/internship-warehouse), specifically fact_content_daily_performance for a mid-panel development month (March 2026: 9,841,378 rows, 55 clients, 331,437 content items), used to validate the data contract and table grain before modeling on the smaller starter slice.

Deliberately excluded

  • Pages below the impression floor — without it, top-ranked pages were dominated by 1–4 impression pages with a mechanical 0% CTR (noise, not signal).
  • Any FlyRank product decision flag (health_score, priority_score, action_type) — never shipped in this dataset by design, and never reconstructed.
  • Warehouse rows without confirmed GA4 tracking, to avoid mistaking "not yet tracked" for "no traffic."

All identifiers are pseudonymized. No client names, domains, URLs, or raw queries appear anywhere in this study or its underlying repository.

03 Methodology

Signal validation, before building anything

confirmed

CTR vs. position tier — clean, monotonic decline: 1.484 (top 3) → 0.652 (page 1) → 0.323 (striking distance) → 0.222 (page 3–5) → 0.150 (deep). This matches the logic behind FlyRank's own low_ctr_visible_page product flag.

top 3 · 1.484page 1 · 0.652striking · 0.323page 3–5 · 0.222deep · 0.150
mixed

Staleness vs. decline rate — not monotonic: 51.2% → 61.1% → 46.7% → 60.0% across increasing staleness buckets, with the two oldest buckets carrying very small sample sizes (n=169, n=5). A useful negative result: staleness alone does not cleanly predict decline in this data.

Baseline rule

baseline_action_score = 0.7 × ctr_gap_score + 0.3 × staleness_score — weighted to reflect the signal check above: the confirmed signal (CTR gap) carries more weight than the mixed one (staleness). Reason code: underperforming_ctr_and_stale.

Model

Random Forest (200 trees, depth 6, class-balanced), with Logistic Regression fit alongside as a simple-model check. Features: impressions_90d, avg_position, ctr, content_age_days, days_since_last_update, word_count (median-imputed; missing in ~29% of training rows), and one-hot-encoded position_tier.

Target / proxy: is_declining = (trend_direction == "down") — a current-window bucket, not a forward-validated outcome.

Validation design

Client-holdout split (grouped by client_id, 80/20 — 22 train clients / 6 test clients, zero overlap). This mattered in practice: a naive random split let 27 clients leak across train and test, and produced an inflated Precision@50 of 0.88 — higher than the grouped result precisely because it partly reflected memorization, not generalization.

Leakage audit

No label-derived or future-window field appears among the model's inputs. Feature-to-label correlations ranged from −0.18 to +0.03 — well below the 0.9 threshold that would suggest a feature secretly encodes the label.

04 Results

reversed

The base rate for the positive label is 59.6% — reported here so Precision@50 is never read in isolation. On the identical client-holdout test split, using the identical metric:

MethodPrecision@50
Baseline rule (underperforming_ctr_and_stale)0.90
Logistic Regression0.92
Random Forest0.76

This is the reverse of the starter pipeline's own demonstration (baseline 0.240 → Random Forest 0.740), and it is reported without adjustment.

Why

Where the Random Forest and the baseline disagreed (40 unique picks each): the baseline's unique picks had a 90.0% actual decline rate at an average of 2,958 impressions; the Random Forest's unique picks had only a 72.5% decline rate despite nearly 3× the impressions (8,763). Feature importances confirm the cause — content_age_days (0.26) outranked ctr (0.19) in the forest, pulling weight toward a general traffic/age signal instead of the specific, confirmed CTR-vs-position gap.

05 Limitations

  • The proxy label is a current-window bucket, not a validated future outcome.
  • No causal claim: refreshing a flagged page is not shown to cause recovery — that requires an experiment this data does not provide.
  • No claim is made about Google's ranking algorithm — only observable outcomes were measured.
  • The baseline-vs-model comparison rests on a small test set (6 clients, ~1,260 rows) and should be treated as directional pending a larger or repeated evaluation.
  • Findings come from a 30,000-row anonymized starter sample, not the full ~79M-row warehouse; patterns may differ at scale.

A cluster of top-tier, high-impression pages reporting exactly 0% CTR is statistically unusual for a real top-3 search result — the likely cause is broken tracking or indexing, not weak content. This was split into its own action category rather than folded into generic refresh recommendations.

What should never be automated

  • Publishing AI-rewritten content from the score alone.
  • Deprioritizing or removing low-scoring pages — monitor means "not flagged this cycle," not "worthless."
  • Bulk-actioning tied scores without spot-checking — 24 pages shared an identical score in review, and identical scores do not guarantee an identical root cause.
  • Treating a flag_for_tracking_audit row as a content problem before a human confirms the tracking issue.
